# Down and Out in Arakay

## The Well of Frogs, session one

### I

The caravan never came in. Raiders took it on the road, the way raiders take anything that is not walled and watched, and what came off that road was five souls afoot, carrying what they could, and no great joy in the carrying.

Elowyn was an elf of the Essanin, small and dark and quick, with a thief's tools at his belt and a bow on his shoulder, and the only one of the company who could read a written line. Anglad, whom men called the Faceless, and Bozz, whom they called the Well Endowed, were Sabbateans both: broad and hunched, teeth filed to points, the scars of their coming of age cut into cheek and chest, and neither of them in the pink of health. Dagen was a ranger and a hunter, patient as a snare. And Ed was half an elf and half a scholar of magic, and could not read a word of it, which was a joke the others made and he endured.

Arakay took them in without lifting its head. It had been a trade city once, and when the river died the trade died with it, and after that the city had gone on dying quietly, the way a man dies of a wound he will not look at. Whole quarters stood shuttered and hollow. What was left had gone to slums. Water and bread cost double what they should, and there was no man in the street who did not know it.

### II

They lodged at the Broken Wheel, in the north-east corner of the old quarter. Nook the Older kept it: a small wiry middle-aged man with a nose for a purse, who wanted his money in his hand before he would show a body a bench. The common room was thick with the sort who do not care to be looked at, so nobody looked.

The next night they went hunting work at the Barking Dogs, which had been a fighting pit before it was a tavern, and still had the sand in the floor and the walls of the pit standing. A dwarf called Barking Dog poured behind the board and took no trouble to hide what he thought of his trade. There they fell in with a merchant who gave his name as Wakhar, a man with goods and no iron to guard them, who wanted an escort to the merchant quarter, south-west of the Piazza dei Rospi. Five gold the head. They took it, and a fool's bargain it looked before the day was out.

### III

They went down through as mean a quarter as the city owned, where the ground fell away in steps and the houses leaned together like drunks, and there, at a crossroads, the trap closed.

East of them an alley. West, a street with an awning and a stack of rolled carpets. Behind, a steep stair going back up. And in the middle of it all an oxcart, set square across the way through, which is a thing a man only marks afterwards.

They came out of the alley mouth and the street corner with crossbows, and there is no honest answer to a crossbow at ten paces except to be somewhere else, or to be on the man before he can span it. The first bolts went at the merchant, which the party only understood later.

Elowyn gave them the answer. He went in low and fast while the rest were still deciding, and his steel went home, and that was the first blood spilled that day. The others followed him in and cut down a second, and the rest loosed and missed, or had their bolts turn off a shield, and then broke and ran rather than stand and reload. Men who shoot from hiding are seldom the men to stand.

Their captain was slower. Bozz took him about the waist in a tackle that carried him clean off his feet, and he came down on his back with a Sabbatean atop him and a point at his throat, and lay still, being a man who could count.

### IV

The man Bozz had put on his back was a literator, and the crossbows had not been meant for the party at all. They had been sent for the merchant. A pack of literators had come out to kill Wakhar, and the escort was an inconvenience to be shot through on the way to him.

That was worth knowing, and it was worth more once the prisoner talked. He was a prisoner who meant to go on living, and men of that kind are talkative. Ed went through his pouches first and came out with thirty-six silver, five electrum and eight copper, and it was while he lay trussed in the dirt that the man gave them the truth of the morning. The merchant's name was not Wakhar. Wakhar was a convenience. His name was Kollaro, and he was high in the Barbers' guild, and that was the whole reason the literators had come out with crossbows to put him in the ground.

So the party had saved the life of a man who had lied to them about his name, and had been paid five gold the head for the doing of it.

Then Kollaro showed them what manner of man they had kept alive. He wanted the captain dead. He asked for it plainly, standing over a bound man in the dirt, in the way a man asks a thing of people he believes to be of his own kind.

They refused him. The captain was bound and beaten and no danger to anyone, and there is a difference between killing and murder, and the party knew which side of that line they stood on. They had taken the man's blood and his purse and they would carry him where he was wanted, but they would not open his throat in an alley to please an employer who had not seen fit to give them his right name. They had no wish to go further down a hole another man had dug.

Kollaro did not press it. They carried the captain to Barber's Alley, and the barbers took him, and the barbers were Kollaro's guild and Kollaro was high in it. That was the second thing learned that morning and the worse of the two. The party had thought to play one guild against another. They had spent the morning doing their employer's work instead, keeping him alive at the crossroads and walking his enemy into his own hands.

The fee went up to ten gold the head. Kollaro paid them fifteen apiece and paid it without a word. None of it bought the man their trust. A man who gives a false name at the hiring is a man who expects the job to go wrong, and a man who asks for a throat cut before noon is a man to walk behind, never before. Every bargain after that was struck with one hand on the purse.

He was helpful, too, in the way of a man who wants to be thought useful, and told them of a Chül in the city who would buy gear they had no more use for. They filed it away and said nothing.

### V

Back at the Piazza dei Rospi they had time to look at the thing the place is named for.

The Well of Frogs is old stone, and about its mouth stand four statues of horned bull frogs, the size of dogs, worn soft by weather. Men say the well is older than the city, and men say such things of wells and graves and old women. Men also say it is cursed, and that is a thing a city proves by its feet. Folk crossing that plaza give the well a wide berth without ever admitting they do it, the way a man steps around a grave he will not name.

That night they caroused at the Barking Dogs and let the talk come to them. The urchins, it was said, sometimes toss one of their own down the well. On mist-shrouded nights a thing in the shape of a man walks the neighbourhood. A frog monster lurks in the water below and drags under whoever leans too near. The last soul to draw water there was from the House of Cheap Delights, which is no recommendation at all. North of the plaza stands a ruin where the urchins gather.

Ed would see for himself. He lowered a lantern on a rope and watched it go down past carvings cut in the shaft, frog shapes, row upon row, and no bottom that the light could find. Then one of those carvings lifted its head, or the light did, and a frog's face turned up at him and croaked.

Ed hauled the lantern up and left, and nothing followed him, and he chose to count that a good sign.

### VI

Behind them an old woman had been watching, not yet a crone, which is a distinction that matters in such a quarter. She offered a reading of palms. Dagen gave her his hand, and she told him they would go down into caverns and find death there, and great reward besides, and that he should keep clear of the green water. Then she went her way with two cats at her heels, and if she looked back, no one marked it.

They slept at the Broken Wheel at a coin the head, leaving each purse at fourteen gold and nothing owing.

Nook keeps two children about the place. Wafik is seven and works the common room. Rewen is a girl grown to a laundress, and old enough to tell a stranger just how the two of them came under Nook's roof. The boy had a question for the company: were they joining the Cosurian armies? There was a war, he said, with Easterners, and the Cosurians were winning it. Not a man among them had an answer for him.

### VII

The next day they went out to find the Chül and found something else first: folk at worship about a broken pillar. The stone was white, andesite or near it, and cut into it was a calf kneeling down with a crown of leaves on its head, and offerings heaped at the foot of it. The congregation had the look of people who have found one thing in a dead city to hold to, and they did not invite questions.

Past the pillar the street turned a corner and became a market again, or the ghost of one, and a man was selling sesame cakes hot enough to sting. They bought, and while they ate they talked, and got in the same breath the way to the Chül, which was worth more than the cakes.

They found him. He would buy what they had and sell what they did not, and his prices were the kind that make a man count his own purse twice. The party counted twice and kept their hands in their pockets. Ed did not. Ed bought a stone, and was told that thrown down hard it would make a noise to wake the whole quarter. He put it away as a man puts away a thing he means to use, and he has not yet thrown it.

### VIII

The ruin north of the plaza was full of children, the way a ruin is full of pigeons. The company came in with the cakes still warm and passed them round, which buys more than silver in such a place. The water is not safe, the children said. The frog monster is real. They call it the Splinter, and it takes children, and it takes them down the well.

Word went round as it does among young things with something to trade, and a second pack came out of the stones to deal. The party had crossbows and swords off the men at the crossroads and no use for them. The urchins had what the street knew. Iron for words, cakes atop it, and both sides went away pleased with the bargain.

What the street knew was a name. Lady Denarosa had lost her son to the Splinter. Orlando, the children said, and if any soul in Arakay would pay to have a boy brought back up out of that well, it was her.

It was Elowyn who saw the shape of it, and Elowyn who said it aloud. They were not to go down that well for thanks, nor for a mother's blessing, nor for the sake of a boy none of them had ever clapped eyes on. They were to go to the lady first and be paid for the going: an advance, or part of the price up front, before a rope was knotted or a torch lit. A drowned child is a grief. A child that may yet be living is a debt, and a debt can be collected.

No one argued with him. Dagen had a fortune telling him there was death and great reward under the ground, and Bozz had fourteen gold in his purse and blood on his boots from a morning he had not been paid enough for.

A boy named Dio offered to lead them to her. He was not bought. He was hired, for one silver piece, and he went in front of them through the slums with the gravity of a lad being paid for the first time in his life.

### IX

So they came to the house of Lady Denarosa with a hired child trotting ahead of them and a proposal forming in the mouth of an elf.

Behind them the well stood in its plaza with four stone frogs about the mouth of it, and under the green water something waited that had not yet been given a name to its face. Ahead of them was a woman who had lost a son, a purse that might be opened, and the small ugly arithmetic of being paid before the work.

That is where the day ended, and where this account of it ends.
